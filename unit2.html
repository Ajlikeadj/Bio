Biomolecules as Data Structures

Life is built from four main molecule classes. We treat them as storage, structure and information formats.

6 sessionsCLA-1Unit 2
S1

Carbohydrates and lipids as energy and membrane storage

Carbohydrates (sugars, starch, glycogen, cellulose) are quick energy and structure; glucose is the cell's main fuel. Lipids (fats, oils, phospholipids, steroids) store more energy per gram and form the two-layer membrane (phospholipid bilayer) with water-loving heads outside and water-fearing tails inside. Think of carbs as RAM (fast, short-term) and fats as hard disk (dense, long-term).

In depth

  • Carbohydrates: monosaccharides (glucose, fructose), disaccharides (sucrose, maltose, lactose) joined by glycosidic bonds, polysaccharides (starch in plants, glycogen in animals, cellulose in cell walls). Humans cannot digest cellulose (beta-1,4 links).
  • Lipids: triglycerides (glycerol plus 3 fatty acids), saturated (solid, no double bonds) vs unsaturated (liquid, double bonds), phospholipids, steroids such as cholesterol.
  • Membrane: fluid mosaic model. Phospholipid bilayer with heads out, tails in; cholesterol controls fluidity; proteins transport and signal; glycoproteins help cell recognition (e.g. blood groups).
  • Energy: complete oxidation gives about 4 kcal/g for carbohydrate and 9 kcal/g for fat, so fat is the compact long-term store.

Remember: Fats give about 9 kcal/g, carbs about 4 kcal/g.

S2

Proteins and nucleic acids as functional data structures

Proteins are chains of amino acids that fold into working machines: enzymes, transporters, antibodies, structural fibres. Nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) store and pass on instructions. In computing terms: DNA is the read-only master archive, RNA is a working copy, a protein is the executed program. A protein sequence is like a string; its 3D fold is the object it becomes.

In depth

  • Amino acid: central carbon with amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen and a side chain R. The 20 types are grouped as non-polar, polar, acidic and basic by their R group.
  • Protein roles: enzymes, transport (haemoglobin), defence (antibodies), structure (collagen, keratin), hormones (insulin), movement (actin, myosin).
  • Nucleic acids: nucleotide = sugar + phosphate + base. DNA has deoxyribose and A, T, G, C; RNA has ribose and A, U, G, C. ATP is also a nucleotide.
  • Data view: a protein is a string over 20 letters, DNA is a string over 4 letters (2 bits per base, so the human genome is about 750 MB raw). Stored in FASTA format.

Remember: Central dogma: DNA, then RNA, then protein.

S3

Enzymes as biological catalysts

Enzymes are mostly proteins that speed up reactions without being used up. The substrate fits into the active site (lock and key, or the more accurate induced-fit idea) and the activation energy drops. Speed depends on temperature, pH and substrate amount; too hot and the enzyme denatures. Inhibitors can block them, which is how many drugs work.

In depth

  • Classes (EC numbers): oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases.
  • Michaelis-Menten: v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]). Low Km means high affinity for the substrate; Vmax is reached when all enzyme is saturated.
  • Inhibition: competitive (binds active site, Km rises, Vmax unchanged), non-competitive (binds elsewhere, Vmax falls). Allosteric regulation and feedback inhibition control pathways.
  • Cofactors (metal ions) and coenzymes (vitamin derivatives like NAD) help. Optimum conditions: about 37 C, pH differs (pepsin 2, trypsin 8). Examples: amylase, catalase, DNA polymerase; ACE inhibitors are enzyme-blocking drugs.

Remember: Like a function: takes an input (substrate), returns an output (product), reusable.

S4

Nucleic acid structure and function relationships

DNA is a double helix: two antiparallel strands of nucleotides (sugar, phosphate, base) held by hydrogen bonds, A-T with two bonds and G-C with three. RNA is usually single-stranded, uses ribose and uracil in place of thymine. Types: mRNA carries the message, tRNA brings amino acids, rRNA builds ribosomes. Structure decides function: the stable helix stores data, the flexible single strand works.

In depth

  • Watson and Crick (1953) built the model using Franklin's X-ray data. Chargaff's rules: A equals T and G equals C.
  • B-DNA: right-handed, 10 base pairs per turn, 3.4 angstrom rise per pair, 34 angstrom pitch, 20 angstrom diameter, with major and minor grooves where proteins read the bases. Strands are antiparallel (5' to 3' opposite 3' to 5').
  • RNA: mRNA carries the gene copy (eukaryotes add a 5' cap, poly-A tail and splice out introns), tRNA has a cloverleaf shape with an anticodon, rRNA is the ribosome core. Some RNAs act as enzymes (ribozymes).
  • Melting temperature (Tm) rises with GC content because G-C has 3 hydrogen bonds against 2 for A-T.

Remember: Base pairing is what makes copying and reading possible.

S5

BLAST tool for sequence database queries

BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) takes your DNA or protein sequence and finds similar ones in huge databases such as NCBI's. It breaks the query into short words, finds matches, extends them into alignments and scores them. Look at the E-value (lower means more significant), percent identity and query coverage. It is how you guess what a new gene does: find its known relatives.

In depth

  • How it works: split the query into short words, find matching words in the database, extend them into local alignments, score with a matrix (BLOSUM62 for protein), report the best hits.
  • Programs: blastn (DNA vs DNA), blastp (protein vs protein), blastx (translated DNA vs protein), tblastn, tblastx.
  • Reading the result: E-value is the number of hits expected by chance (below 1e-5 usually significant), bit score is size-independent, percent identity and query coverage show how similar and how much matched.
  • Uses: identify an unknown gene or species, find homologs to guess function, check primers. Limits: heuristic (can miss weak matches) and results depend on the database.

Remember: It is basically Google search for sequences.

S6

Human Genome Project as big data genomics

The Human Genome Project (1990-2003) read all about 3 billion base pairs of human DNA, revealing roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes. It proved that biology needs data storage, assembly algorithms and databases. It started modern genomics, cheap sequencing and personalised medicine, and raised privacy and ethics questions about genetic data.

In depth

  • Timeline: launched 1990 (US DOE and NIH with UK, Japan, France, Germany, China), draft in 2001, declared complete in 2003; the remaining gaps were closed by the telomere-to-telomere project in 2022.
  • Methods: hierarchical shotgun (public project) and whole-genome shotgun (Celera). Software had to assemble millions of short reads, a huge computing problem.
  • Findings: about 3 billion base pairs, roughly 20,000-25,000 genes, only about 1.5 percent codes for protein, and any two people are about 99.9 percent identical.
  • Impact: data stored in GenBank, Ensembl and UCSC browser; led to next-generation sequencing, personalised medicine and falling cost (billions then, under $1000 now). About 5 percent of the budget went to ethical, legal and social issues.

Remember: Raw data in; assembly, annotation and analysis software out.
