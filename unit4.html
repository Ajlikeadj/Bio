AI/ML Foundations in Biology

Learn the machine learning basics needed to work with biological data.

6 sessionsCLA-2Unit 4
S1

Overview of AI for biological big data

Biology now produces huge data: genomes, proteomes, images, patient records. AI means machines doing tasks that need intelligence; machine learning (ML) is the part that learns patterns from data; deep learning is ML using many-layered neural networks. Uses include gene finding, disease prediction, protein folding and image analysis.

In depth

  • AI = machines doing tasks needing intelligence; ML = learning patterns from data; deep learning = ML with many-layered neural networks.
  • Biological big data has volume, variety (sequences, structures, images, health records) and velocity (fast sequencers). GenBank and other databases keep growing rapidly.
  • Tasks: classification, prediction, clustering, generation. Examples: DeepVariant for variant calling, AlphaFold for structure.
  • Why now: more data, powerful GPUs, better algorithms.

Remember: AI contains ML, which contains deep learning.

S2

Supervised and unsupervised learning basics

Supervised learning trains on labelled examples (tumour or normal) to predict labels: classification (categories) and regression (numbers). Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabelled data: clustering groups similar genes or patients, and PCA reduces dimensions. Models are split into training and test sets to check they generalise; overfitting means memorising instead of learning.

In depth

  • Supervised algorithms: linear and logistic regression, decision trees, random forest, SVM, k-nearest neighbours, neural networks. Classification gives a category; regression gives a number.
  • Unsupervised: k-means and hierarchical clustering, PCA, t-SNE or UMAP. Example: clustering expression data to discover cancer subtypes.
  • Data split: training (fit), validation (tune), test (final check), e.g. 70/15/15. Overfitting = great on training, poor on new data; underfitting = too simple. Regularisation reduces overfitting.
  • Also exist: semi-supervised and reinforcement learning.

Remember: Labels given = supervised. No labels = unsupervised.

S3

Feature representations for sequences and omics

Models need numbers. DNA can be one-hot encoded (A=1000, C=0100...), split into k-mers (short substrings) or embedded by learned vectors. Omics data (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics) become a matrix: rows are samples, columns are features like gene expression. Good features matter more than the algorithm.

In depth

  • One-hot encoding turns A, C, G, T into vectors like 1000. k-mer counts treat sequences as words (k=3 gives 64 features). Learned embeddings and protein language models (e.g. ESM) give richer vectors.
  • Omics: genomics (DNA), transcriptomics (RNA), proteomics, metabolomics. Data forms a matrix with samples as rows and genes as columns.
  • Curse of dimensionality: far more features than samples. Fix with feature selection (variance, LASSO) or PCA.
  • Normalise first (e.g. TPM, log transform) so large values do not dominate.

Remember: Normalise and select features first, to avoid 20,000 columns for 50 samples.

S4

AI data pipeline: preprocess, train, evaluate

Pipeline: collect data, clean it (missing values, outliers, batch effects), preprocess (scale, encode), split train/validation/test, train a model, tune hyperparameters, evaluate. Metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC. Use cross-validation for small datasets, and never let test data leak into training.

In depth

  • Steps: collect, clean (missing values, outliers, batch effects), preprocess (scale, encode), split, train, tune hyperparameters (grid search), evaluate, deploy and monitor.
  • Confusion matrix: TP, FP, TN, FN. Precision = TP/(TP+FP); recall (sensitivity) = TP/(TP+FN); F1 is their harmonic mean; ROC-AUC measures ranking quality.
  • Cross-validation (k-fold) is used for small datasets. Imbalanced data needs class weights or SMOTE. Data leakage (test info reaching training) gives falsely good results.
  • Tools: Python with pandas and scikit-learn.

Remember: Precision = how many predicted positives are right; recall = how many real positives are found.

S5

Applications in genomics and systems biology

In genomics, ML predicts gene variants' effects, finds promoters and classifies cancer types. In systems biology, it models gene regulatory networks, metabolic pathways and how many components interact; it helps predict cell behaviour after a drug or mutation. Network and graph methods represent genes and proteins as nodes with links.

In depth

  • Genomics uses: variant effect prediction, gene and promoter finding, GWAS, cancer type classification, CRISPR guide design.
  • Systems biology studies the whole network: gene regulatory networks, signalling and metabolic pathways (flux balance analysis). Genes and proteins are nodes, interactions are edges, hubs are highly connected nodes.
  • Combine ordinary differential equation models with ML; cluster single-cell RNA-seq data into cell types; predict drug response of a cell line.
  • Goal: a computational 'digital twin' of a cell to test interventions.

Remember: Systems biology looks at the whole network, not one gene.

S6

Benefits and limitations of AI in biology

Benefits: speed, finding patterns humans miss, cheaper screening, scalable analysis. Limitations: needs large, good-quality labelled data; bias if data lack diversity; black-box models are hard to explain; results may not generalise; lab validation is still required. AI supports scientists, it doesn't replace experiments.

In depth

  • Benefits: speed, scalability, finding hidden patterns, cheaper screening, earlier diagnosis.
  • Limits: garbage in, garbage out; small samples with many features; batch effects; biased data; black-box models; irreproducible results; heavy compute and energy use.
  • Mitigation: diverse data, external validation on new cohorts, explainable AI (SHAP, saliency maps), keeping a human in the loop, and confirming findings by experiment.
  • Key idea: a model finds correlation, biology needs causation.

Remember: Correlation found by a model is not proof of a biological cause.
