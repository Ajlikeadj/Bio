Protein Synthesis: From Code to Protein

How does a cell convert genetic information into a functional protein?

Central idea:

DNA → RNA → Protein

  • DNA stores the instructions.
  • RNA carries the required information.
  • Ribosomes read the information.
  • Amino acids are assembled into a protein.
  • The amino acid sequence determines the protein's primary structure.

Two major processes:

  • Transcription:
    DNA → RNA
  • Translation:
    RNA → Protein
  • This provides a simple framework for understanding how information stored in DNA ultimately produces a functional protein.

Transcription: DNA → mRNA

  • Transcription is the process of producing an RNA molecule using DNA as a template.
  • The enzyme RNA polymerase plays a central role.

Basic process:

  • DNA gene → RNA polymerase → mRNA
  • The resulting messenger RNA (mRNA) carries the genetic information needed for protein synthesis.
  • In eukaryotes, transcription occurs in the nucleus.

Why mRNA?

  • DNA is the stable storage molecule, while mRNA acts as a temporary working copy of the required information.

What is mRNA?

  • mRNA = messenger RNA
  • It carries genetic information from DNA to the ribosome.
  • RNA contains four bases:
  • A – Adenine
    U – Uracil
    G – Guanine
    C – Cytosine
  • DNA → T (Thymine) instead of RNA → U (Uracil)
  • The sequence of bases in mRNA determines the order in which amino acids will be incorporated into the protein.
  • The initial transcript from a protein-coding gene is pre-mRNA.

Pre-mRNA Formation

  • Pre-mRNA is the primary RNA transcript produced from a protein-coding gene.
  • It contains sequences that will be retained and sequences that will be removed during processing.
  • Pre-mRNA undergoes processing before becoming mature mRNA.
  • Major processing events are 5′ capping, splicing, and polyadenylation.

Pre-mRNA Processing

  • 5′ capping adds a modified guanine nucleotide to the 5′ end.
  • Splicing removes introns and joins exons.
  • Polyadenylation adds a poly-A tail to the 3′ end.
  • Processing produces mature mRNA suitable for export to the cytoplasm and translation

RNA Splicing: Introns and Exons

  • Introns are intervening sequences removed from pre-mRNA.
  • Exons are sequences retained in mature RNA.
  • The spliceosome protein carries out the major steps of pre-mRNA splicing.
  • Exons are joined to form the mature mRNA sequence.

Mature mRNA

  • Mature mRNA contains a 5′ cap, joined exons, and a 3′ poly-A tail.
  • It is transported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm in eukaryotic cells.
  • Mature mRNA provides the template for protein synthesis at the ribosome.

Translation: mRNA → Protein

  • Translation is the process by which the information in mRNA is converted into an amino acid sequence.
  • Translation occurs at the ribosome.
  • The ribosome: binds to mRNA.
  • Reads the mRNA sequence.
  • Identifies codons.
  • Brings together the appropriate amino acids.
  • Links the amino acids using peptide bonds.
  • Produces a polypeptide chain.

Ribosome Structure and Function

  • Ribosomes are molecular machines responsible for translation.
  • They consist of a small and a large subunit.
  • The small subunit participates in mRNA recognition and decoding.
  • The large subunit contains the catalytic center involved in peptide bond formation.

Ribosomal A, P and E Sites

  • A site: accepts incoming aminoacyl-tRNA.
  • P site: holds the tRNA carrying the growing polypeptide.
  • E site: accommodates the exiting deacylated tRNA.
  • These sites coordinate sequential addition of amino acids

Codons: Reading the Genetic Code

  • The ribosome reads mRNA in groups of three nucleotides.
  • Each group of three is called a codon.
  • Example:
  • AUG – GCU – AAA – UGC
  • Each codon specifies an amino acid or provides a start/stop signal.
  • Because there are four possible RNA bases:
  • 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 possible codons
  • These codons specify the 20 standard amino acids, along with start and stop signals.
  • Think of codons as three-character instructions in a biological programming language.

  • tRNA helps bring the correct amino acid to the ribosome during translation.
  • Each tRNA contains:
  • An anticodon
  • A corresponding amino acid

Translation Process : Initiation

  • Translation begins when the ribosome assembles on mRNA.
  • The start codon, usually AUG, establishes the reading frame.
  • The initiator tRNA carrying methionine pairs with the start codon.
  • The complete ribosome is assembled and ready for elongation.

Translation: Elongation

  • An aminoacyl-tRNA enters the A site and pairs with the corresponding codon.
  • A peptide bond is formed between amino acids.
  • The ribosome moves along mRNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
  • tRNAs move through the A, P and E sites as the polypeptide grows.

Translation: Termination

  • Translation terminates when a stop codon enters the ribosome.
  • Stop codons do not encode amino acids.
  • Release factors promote release of the completed polypeptide.
  • Ribosomal subunits and mRNA can subsequently dissociate.

What is Primary Structure?

  • The primary structure of a protein is the specific linear sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain.

For example:

Met – Ala – Gly – Lys – Leu – Ser – Val

  • The amino acids are connected by peptide bonds.
  • The sequence is directional: N-terminus → C-terminus

Why is it important?

  • The amino acid sequence provides the foundation for the protein's higher-order structure and ultimately influences its function.

What are Proteins?

  • Proteins are complex biological molecules made up of smaller units called amino acids.
  • They perform a wide range of functions:
  • Enzymes – catalyze biochemical reactions
  • Structural proteins – provide support
  • Transport proteins – carry molecules
  • Receptors – receive signals
  • Antibodies – participate in defense
  • Regulatory proteins – control cellular processes

Figure

  • Proteins are constructed from 20 standard amino acids.
  • Each amino acid contains:
  • Amino group (–NH₂)
  • Carboxyl group (–COOH)
  • Hydrogen atom
  • Variable R group
  • Central carbon atom
  • The R group differs among amino acids and gives each amino acid its unique chemical properties.

Complete notes

Figure

  • Secondary structure describes the local folding patterns of the polypeptide backbone.
  • The major types are:
  • α-helix-A coiled, spiral arrangement of the polypeptide backbone.
  • β-sheet-Extended sections of the polypeptide chain arranged next to one another.
  • Both are stabilized mainly by hydrogen bonds involving the peptide backbone.

α-helix

  • An α-helix is a regular helical structure formed by twisting of the polypeptide backbone.
  • Stabilized mainly by hydrogen bonds.
  • Hydrogen bonds occur between groups in the peptide backbone.
  • Amino acid side chains project outward from the helix.
  • A protein can contain several α-helical regions.

β-sheet

  • A β-sheet is formed when extended sections of a polypeptide chain, called β-strands, are positioned next to one another.
  • Stabilized by hydrogen bonds between peptide backbones.
  • β-strands can be arranged as:
  • Parallel
  • Antiparallel

Turns and Loops

  • Proteins also contain:
  • Turns
  • Loops
  • Irregular structural regions
  • These regions connect different secondary structural elements and allow the polypeptide chain to change direction.

Tertiary Structure

  • Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide chain.
  • It describes how different secondary structural elements are positioned relative to one another.

For example:

  • α-helix + β-sheet + loops + turns → Complete 3D structure
  • Tertiary structure creates functional regions such as:
  • Active sites
  • Binding sites
  • Interaction surfaces

Factors Stabilising Tertiary Structure

  • Hydrophobic interactions
    Non-polar groups tend to become buried within the protein.
  • Hydrogen bonds
    Form between suitable polar groups.
  • Ionic interactions
    Occur between oppositely charged groups.
  • Disulfide bonds
    Can form between cysteine residues.
  • Van der Waals interactions
    Contribute through close contact between atoms.

Figure

Protein Domain

  • A protein domain is a distinct structural and often functional region within a protein.
  • A protein may contain: One domain or multiple domains
  • Different domains may perform different functions.

For example:

  • Domain 1 → Molecular binding
  • Domain 2 → Catalytic activity
  • Domains are important for understanding the relationship between protein structure and function.

Quaternary Structure

  • Some proteins contain more than one polypeptide chain.
  • The arrangement of these individual chains into a functional protein is called quaternary structure.
  • The individual chains are called subunits.

For example:

  • Subunit A + Subunit B + Subunit C → Functional protein
  • Each subunit may have its own tertiary structure.

Example: Hemoglobin

  • Hemoglobin is an important example of a protein with quaternary structure.
  • It consists of four polypeptide subunits:
  • 2 α subunits + 2 β subunits
  • Each subunit contains a heme group.
  • The four subunits work together in the functional hemoglobin molecule, which is involved in oxygen transport.

A subunit is an individual polypeptide chain within a protein complex. A domain is a distinct structural and often functional region within a polypeptide chain. Hemoglobin has four polypeptide subunits—two α and two β. Each globin subunit has a characteristic globin structural fold and binds one heme group.

Figure

  • PyMOL is a molecular visualization software used to view biological molecules in three dimensions.
  • It can be used to visualize:
  • Proteins
  • DNA and RNA
  • Small molecules
  • Protein–ligand complexes

Why Visualize Proteins?

  • Protein function is closely related to its three-dimensional structure.
  • A protein sequence alone does not easily show:
  • How the protein is folded
  • Where α-helices and β-sheets are located
  • Where ligands bind
  • How different chains are arranged
  • 3D visualization helps us see and understand protein structure.

From Protein Data to 3D Structure

  • A protein structure contains information about the position of atoms in three-dimensional space.
  • Each atom has coordinates: X – Y – Z
  • The source of the coordinates for Protein Structure is the RCSB PDB
  • The Protein Data Bank (PDB) is a freely accessible database that stores the three-dimensional structures of biological macromolecules, mainly proteins, nucleic acids and their complexes.
  • PyMOL uses these coordinates to display the molecule.

SOURCE OF 3D structures

  • PDB-RCSB PDB: Homepage
  • Search PDB → Select structure → Check structure information → Download PDB/mmCIF file → Visualize or analyse in Pymol
  • Try the PDB code 1HHO- retrive the PDB file of Hemoglobin

Protein Structure in PyMOL

  • PyMOL can display a protein using different representations.
  • Common representations:
  • Cartoon – overall protein structure
  • Sticks – atoms and bonds
  • Spheres – individual atoms
  • Surface – outer molecular shape
  • Different representations highlight different features of the same protein.
  • Other than the said representations there are many more types of representations of proteins available in PyMol and other tools

Cartoon – Overall Protein Structure

  • The cartoon representation is a simplified way of displaying the overall folding pattern of a protein.
  • Instead of showing every atom, PyMOL represents the protein backbone using ribbons and arrows.
  • α-helices appear as coiled or spiral structures.
  • β-sheets appear as broad arrows.
  • Loops and turns appear as flexible connecting lines or ribbons.
  • Individual atoms are generally not shown.

Why is it useful?

  • It allows us to quickly understand the secondary structure and overall 3D organisation of a protein without being distracted by thousands of atoms.

Sticks – Atoms and Bonds

  • The stick representation shows individual atoms and the bonds connecting them.
  • Atoms are represented as small shapes, while bonds appear as cylindrical sticks between atoms.
  • For example, if we want to examine a small region of a protein:
  • Amino acid → atoms → bonds → molecular arrangement
  • Sticks provide much more detail than the cartoon representation.

Why is it useful?

  • It is particularly useful when looking closely at:
  • Specific amino acids
  • Active-site residues
  • Ligands
  • Protein–ligand interactions
  • Hydrogen-bonding or other close-contact regions

Spheres – Individual Atoms

  • In the sphere representation, atoms are displayed as spherical objects.
  • The size of the spheres can represent the approximate size or spatial occupation of atoms, depending on the display setting.
  • When many atoms are shown as spheres, the protein can appear like a collection of tightly packed balls.

Why is it useful?

  • It helps us appreciate:
  • The physical space occupied by atoms
  • Atomic arrangement
  • How closely atoms are packed
  • The approximate size and shape of individual atoms
  • However, showing an entire large protein as spheres can make the structure look crowded and difficult to interpret.

Surface – Outer Molecular Shape

  • The surface representation displays the approximate outer boundary of the molecule.
  • Instead of seeing individual atoms and bonds, we see the molecular surface surrounding them.
  • Imagine placing a flexible sheet around the outside of the protein—the resulting shape represents its molecular surface.
  • It allows us to see features such as:
  • Protein shape
  • Grooves
  • Cavities
  • Pockets
  • Surface-exposed regions

Why is it useful?

  • Surface representation is particularly helpful for understanding where another molecule might interact with the protein.
  • For example, a drug molecule may fit into a pocket on the protein surface.

Complete notes

Secondary Structure Prediction

  • Proteins are long chains of amino acids that fold into specific three-dimensional structures.
  • Secondary structure prediction is the computational prediction of local structural elements from an amino acid sequence.
  • Major structural elements
  • α-helix
  • β-strand / β-sheet
  • Loops and turns

Classification of Prediction Methods

  • Secondary structure prediction methods can be broadly classified according to the type of information used for prediction.
  • Three major approaches
  • Sequence-based methods
    → Use information from the target amino acid sequence
  • Homology-based methods
    → Use information from related proteins with known structures
  • Hybrid methods
    → Combine sequence, evolutionary/homology information and computational learning

Basic Principle of Prediction

  • Different amino acids have different tendencies to occur in different structural environments.
  • Prediction methods examine:
  • Amino acid composition
  • Sequence patterns
  • Neighbouring residues
  • Evolutionary information
  • Conserved regions
  • These features are used to estimate the most likely secondary structure at each position.

What are Sequence-Based Methods?

  • Sequence-based methods predict secondary structure directly from the amino acid sequence.
  • They examine properties and patterns within the sequence to predict whether a region is likely to form:
  • α-helix | β-strand | Coil
  • Basic workflow

  • These methods do not require a structurally characterized homolog.

Examples of Sequence-Based Methods

Classical methods

  • Chou–Fasman – uses amino acid structural propensities
  • GOR (Garnier–Osguthorpe–Robson) – considers neighbouring residues and statistical information

Machine-learning approaches

  • SVM-based predictors
  • Neural-network-based predictors
  • Main advantage
  • They can make predictions even when a suitable homologous structure is unavailable.
  • Limitation
  • A single sequence may not contain enough information to accurately predict every structural feature.

  • PredictProtein
  • HHpred
  • Major advantage
  • When a close structural homolog is available, prediction can be highly reliable.
  • Major limitation
  • Performance decreases when there is no suitable homolog or when sequence similarity is very low.

What are Hybrid Methods?

  • Hybrid methods combine information from multiple sources rather than relying on a single type of information.
  • They may combine:
  • Amino acid sequence
  • Local sequence patterns
  • Evolutionary information
  • Multiple sequence alignments
  • Sequence profiles
  • Machine learning/deep learning
  • More information → better representation of the protein → improved prediction

  • JPred

  • DeepCNF
  • SPOT-1D

  • Advantages
  • Combines complementary information
  • Generally more powerful than relying on one information source
  • Well suited to modern ML/deep-learning approaches
  • Limitations
  • More computationally complex
  • Dependent on the quality of evolutionary/profile information

Complete notes

Introduction to Drug Design

  • Drug design is the process of discovering and developing molecules that can interact with a specific biological target to produce a desired therapeutic effect.
  • Most drug targets are biological molecules such as proteins, enzymes, receptors and ion channels.
  • The basic concept is:
  • Disease → Identify biological target → Find/design drug molecule → Drug–target interaction → Desired therapeutic effect
  • A drug must not only bind to its target but should ideally show good potency, selectivity, safety and drug-like properties.

Drug Targets and Drug–Target Interactions

  • A drug target is a biological molecule whose activity can be modified by a drug to produce a beneficial effect.
  • Common targets( mostly proteins) include
  • Enzymes – drugs may inhibit or activate their activity
  • Receptors – drugs can activate or block cellular signalling
  • Ion channels – drugs can influence the movement of ions
  • Transporters – drugs can alter the movement of molecules across membranes
  • Drugs interact with their targets through interactions such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions and van der Waals forces.
  • A binding site is the region of the target where the drug interacts. Its shape and chemical properties strongly influence drug binding.

Approaches and Computational Methods

Two important approaches are used in drug design.

  • Structure-Based Drug Design (SBDD)
  • Uses the 3D structure of the target protein to identify binding pockets and design or select molecules that can interact with them.
  • Target structure → Binding site → Compound screening/design → Docking → Candidate molecules
  • Ligand-Based Drug Design (LBDD)
  • Used when the target structure is unavailable but compounds with known biological activity are available.
  • Known active compounds → Identify common features → Build model → Design/search for new compounds

Computational techniques

  • Molecular docking – predicts possible drug–target binding poses
  • Virtual screening – computationally screens large compound libraries
  • Molecular dynamics – studies molecular movement and stability
  • QSAR – relates chemical properties to biological activity
  • AI/ML – predicts activity and assists in compound discovery
