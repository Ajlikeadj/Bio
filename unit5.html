Deep Learning, Healthcare and Ethics

Neural networks in medicine, and the responsibilities that come with them.

6 sessionsCLA-2Unit 5
S1

Deep learning for images, signals and sequences

A neural network has layers of connected nodes with weights learned by backpropagation. CNNs (convolutional) find patterns in images such as X-rays and microscope slides. RNNs, LSTMs and transformers handle sequences like DNA, proteins and text. ECG and EEG signals are time-series, handled by 1D CNNs or sequence models.

In depth

  • Neuron: weighted sum of inputs plus bias passed through an activation (ReLU, sigmoid, softmax). Layers stack neurons; a loss function measures error; gradient descent with backpropagation updates weights over many epochs.
  • CNN: convolution filters detect edges and patterns, pooling shrinks the image, fully connected layers classify. Used for X-rays, slides, microscopy. A 1D CNN can find DNA motifs.
  • RNN and LSTM process sequences step by step; transformers use attention to relate all positions and power protein language models. ECG and EEG are time-series signals.
  • Also: transfer learning (reuse a trained model), autoencoders, GANs. Needs large data and GPUs.

Remember: Images: CNN. Sequences: RNN or transformer.

S2

AI in drug discovery and biotechnology

AI predicts protein structure (AlphaFold), screens millions of compounds virtually, designs new molecules (generative models), predicts toxicity, and suggests drug repurposing. In biotech it helps design enzymes, optimise fermentation and engineer crops. This cuts the years and cost of finding a drug candidate.

In depth

  • Drug discovery stages helped by AI: target finding, virtual screening of millions of molecules, generative design (VAEs, GANs, diffusion), ADMET prediction, repurposing existing drugs.
  • AlphaFold database provides predicted structures for over 200 million proteins, speeding up target analysis. AI-designed candidates have entered clinical trials.
  • Biotechnology: enzyme engineering, CRISPR guide prediction, synthetic biology design, fermentation optimisation, crop improvement.
  • Value: cuts cost and years from early discovery, but lab and clinical validation are still essential.

Remember: Shortlist with AI, confirm in the lab.

S3

AI-assisted medical diagnostics and imaging

Deep learning reads chest X-rays, CT, MRI, retina scans and pathology slides to detect pneumonia, tumours, diabetic retinopathy and more, often matching specialists on narrow tasks. It acts as a second opinion, flags urgent cases and reduces workload, while the doctor makes the final decision.

In depth

  • Data: X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, retinal fundus images, histopathology slides, dermoscopy. Tasks: classification, detection, segmentation (U-Net), report generation.
  • Example: automated diabetic retinopathy screening approved by the US FDA in 2018.
  • Metrics: sensitivity (finds disease), specificity (avoids false alarms). Heat maps like Grad-CAM show where the model looked.
  • Issues: models fail on data from a different hospital (dataset shift), need regulatory approval (FDA, CDSCO), and must stay a support tool. Helpful where specialists are scarce, such as rural areas.

Remember: Tools are 'decision support', not a doctor replacement.

S4

AI for integrated bioinformatics and health data

Patient care generates electronic health records, genomic data, lab values, wearables and images. Integrating these (multi-omics plus clinical data) allows precision medicine: predicting disease risk, choosing a therapy for a patient's genetic profile. Challenges are different formats, missing data and privacy.

In depth

  • Sources: electronic health records, genomes, imaging, lab tests, wearables. Standards help integration: FHIR and HL7 for records, DICOM for images.
  • Precision medicine: pick drug and dose using the patient's genes, e.g. CYP2C19 variants affect clopidogrel response. Multi-omics plus clinical data improves risk prediction.
  • Techniques: knowledge graphs, and federated learning, where the model trains at each hospital so raw data never leaves.
  • Challenges: missing values, different formats, privacy, interoperability. India's Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission is building such a health data system.

Remember: Combine many data types for a fuller patient picture.

S5

Real-time AI for monitoring and decision support

Sensors and wearables stream heart rate, glucose and oxygen. Models detect abnormal patterns live: ICU early-warning for sepsis, arrhythmia alerts, insulin dosing support. Needs low latency, reliability and few false alarms, since too many alerts cause alarm fatigue.

In depth

  • Wearables and bedside sensors stream heart rate, oxygen and glucose. Models flag abnormal patterns as they happen, often on the device itself (edge computing).
  • Examples: ICU sepsis early warning, smartwatch arrhythmia alerts, closed-loop insulin delivery (artificial pancreas), outbreak surveillance.
  • Requirements: low latency, reliability, few false alarms (too many cause alarm fatigue), explanations the clinician can check.
  • Decision support presents suggestions; the clinician stays responsible.

Remember: Speed and trust are as important as accuracy.

S6

Ethical and social issues of AI in biology

Key issues: patient privacy and consent, data security, bias that harms under-represented groups, explainability, who is responsible if AI errs, fair access to technology, and misuse (such as dangerous biological design). Principles: fairness, transparency, accountability, human oversight, and following rules like HIPAA, GDPR and India's DPDP Act.

In depth

  • Privacy and consent: health and genetic data are sensitive; anonymisation can fail; people must know how data is used. Genetic discrimination (insurance, jobs) is a risk.
  • Bias and fairness: training data mostly from European ancestry or certain skin tones gives worse results for others. Accountability: who is responsible when AI errs? Transparency: can the decision be explained?
  • Access and misuse: digital divide in who gets AI care; dual-use risk where tools could help design harmful biological agents.
  • Rules: HIPAA (US), GDPR (EU), India's DPDP Act 2023, ICMR ethical guidelines for AI in health. Principles: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice.

Remember: Ask 'who benefits, who is harmed, who is accountable?'
